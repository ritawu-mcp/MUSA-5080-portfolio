The cross-validation helper

This function runs 10-fold cross-validation with the same seed every time, so every model in your log is tested on the same folds.

Code 
ctrl <- trainControl(method = "cv", number = 10)

cv_rmse <- function(formula, data) {
  df <- if (inherits(data, "sf")) st_drop_geometry(data) else data
  vars <- all.vars(formula)
  bad <- vars[sapply(vars, function(v) any(is.na(df[[v]])))]
  if (length(bad) > 0) {
    stop("These variables have NAs: ", paste(bad, collapse = ", "),
         ". Fill them or choose another variable (Rule 1: don't drop rows).")
  }
  set.seed(5080)
  fit <- train(formula, data = df, method = "lm", trControl = ctrl)
  fit$results$RMSE
}

Part 1: Choose your outcome

The poverty rate reflects the economic conditions of people in a community. A high poverty rate is important to consider in long-range planning, especially for equity planning. When planning for public investment, job assistance, and housing support, areas with higher poverty rates may need more attention and resources.

1A:outcome and baseline predictors

the 3 baseline predictors i choose are：unemployment rate, median household income, and percentage of housing units built before 1940. Why these 3 variables are relevant?

  • Unemployment rate: It shows the job conditions in an area. I expect areas with higher unemployment to have higher poverty rates.
  • Median household income: It is a strong indicator of the socioeconomic conditions of an area. Areas with lower household incomes may also have higher poverty rates.
  • Housing units built before 1940: It describes the age of the housing stock and can be used as a proxy for broader neighborhood conditions that may be related to poverty.

The poverty rate is an important outcome for planning because it reflects the economic conditions of people in a community. The feature that helped the most was the log-transformed drug density when it was combined with unemployment rate, median household income, and school-distance categories. The final model reduced CV RMSE from 9.63 for the baseline model to 8.99. In contrast, the original spatial-feature model had a higher CV RMSE of 10.57, so adding spatial variables does not automatically improve prediction. The drug variables may overlap with information already captured by unemployment and income, while the log transformation helped reduce the influence of tracts with very high drug-incident densities. 、

For example, the coefficient for unemployment rate in the final model is 0.254. The β̂ interpretation is that, holding the other variables constant, a one-percentage-point increase in the unemployment rate is associated with about a 0.254 percentage-point increase in predicted poverty rate. In terms of ŷ, if two otherwise similar tracts differ by one percentage point in unemployment, the model predicts the tract with higher unemployment to have a poverty rate about 0.254 percentage points higher.

The residual map shows that some spatial patterns remain, especially around Center City and North Philadelphia. One thing I would want to measure next is access to other neighborhood resources and services, such as transit, food stores, or community facilities. These data could come from Philadelphia’s open data portal or other city and regional open-data sources.

Finally, the last plot shows that low ACS CV does not necessarily mean a small prediction error. Some tracts with relatively reliable poverty estimates still have large residuals. ### The cross-validation helper

This function runs 10-fold cross-validation with the same seed every time, so every model in your log is tested on the same folds. 

```{r}
#| label: cv-helper
ctrl <- trainControl(method = "cv", number = 10)

cv_rmse <- function(formula, data) {
  df <- if (inherits(data, "sf")) st_drop_geometry(data) else data
  vars <- all.vars(formula)
  bad <- vars[sapply(vars, function(v) any(is.na(df[[v]])))]
  if (length(bad) > 0) {
    stop("These variables have NAs: ", paste(bad, collapse = ", "),
         ". Fill them or choose another variable (Rule 1: don't drop rows).")
  }
  set.seed(5080)
  fit <- train(formula, data = df, method = "lm", trControl = ctrl)
  fit$results$RMSE
}
```

## Part 7: Reflection 

The feature that helped the most was the log-transformed drug density when it was combined with unemployment rate, median household income, and school-distance categories. The final model reduced CV RMSE from 9.63 for the baseline model to 8.99. In contrast, the original spatial-feature model had a higher CV RMSE of 10.57, so adding spatial variables does not automatically improve prediction. The drug variables may overlap with information already captured by unemployment and income, while the log transformation helped reduce the influence of tracts with very high drug-incident densities. 
、

For example, the coefficient for unemployment rate in the final model is 0.254. The β̂ interpretation is that, holding the other variables constant, a one-percentage-point increase in the unemployment rate is associated with about a 0.254 percentage-point increase in predicted poverty rate. In terms of ŷ, if two otherwise similar tracts differ by one percentage point in unemployment, the model predicts the tract with higher unemployment to have a poverty rate about 0.254 percentage points higher.

The residual map shows that some spatial patterns remain, especially around Center City and North Philadelphia. One thing I would want to measure next is access to other neighborhood resources and services, such as transit, food stores, or community facilities. These data could come from Philadelphia's open data portal or other city and regional open-data sources.

Finally, the last plot shows that low ACS CV does not necessarily mean a small prediction error. Some tracts with relatively reliable poverty estimates still have large residuals. This suggests that the remaining error is not simply caused by ACS sampling uncertainty; some of it likely reflects neighborhood characteristics that are not included in the model.
